The Lions of Tsavo

A True Story

🎧 Listen to the Story

In 1898, British engineers were building a railway bridge over the Tsavo River in Kenya. Thousands of workers had gathered in a remote area surrounded by wilderness. They expected difficult conditions, but they did not expect to be hunted. Soon after construction began, workers started disappearing during the night. Witnesses reported seeing two large lions entering the camps and dragging people away. At first, many believed the attacks were isolated incidents. They were wrong.

The lions became increasingly bold. They ignored campfires and broke through thorn fences that were supposed to keep predators away. Panic spread among the workers. Some refused to sleep at night, while others abandoned the project entirely. The man in charge of the bridge was Lieutenant Colonel John Henry Patterson. Determined to stop the attacks, he organized hunting parties and set traps. However, the lions seemed unusually intelligent. Again and again, they avoided capture.

For months, the attacks continued. Workers told terrifying stories of hearing screams in the darkness and finding only footprints the next morning. The railway project slowed dramatically as fear gripped the camp. Finally, Patterson decided to hunt the lions himself. After several dangerous encounters, he managed to kill the first lion. The second continued attacking for weeks before Patterson tracked it down and shot it as well.

The killings finally stopped, and construction resumed. Estimates vary, but dozens of people may have been killed during the attacks. The story became famous around the world and inspired books and films. Even today, the preserved lions can be seen in a museum in Chicago.

Scientists still debate why the lions attacked so many humans. Some believe illness prevented them from hunting normal prey. Others think environmental changes reduced their food supply. Whatever the reason, the lions of Tsavo remain one of the most frightening examples of man-eaters in recorded history. The story leaves us wondering: Would you have stayed and worked, or would fear have driven you to flee?
